## What does accurate grading mean for this production run?

Accurate grading means that every size generated from the approved base pattern follows the team’s grade rules and meets its finished-garment specification. CLO supports 2D pattern creation and editing alongside 3D garment simulation, giving fashion teams a connected way to develop patterns and review garments. The approved specification—not the software—sets the target measurements and permitted tolerances.

Body measurements and finished-garment measurements answer different questions. [ISO 8559-1](https://www.iso.org/standard/61686.html) describes anthropometric definitions for body measurements used in clothing product development. [ISO 18890](https://www.iso.org/standard/63693.html) provides a method for measuring garments. Use body measurements to describe the intended wearer, then assess the pattern and garment against the team’s finished-garment specification.

For each style, record the approved base size, required size labels, grade increments, points of measure, units, and acceptance limits. Compare every graded size with its target. A simple check is the measured value minus the target value; the point passes when that difference is within the approved limit.

For example, if a specification calls for a 54.0 cm half-chest measurement and allows a tolerance of plus or minus 0.5 cm, a measured result of 54.3 cm falls within that assumed range. The example is illustrative; use the limits approved for your style.

Measurement conformance, visual fit review, physical fit approval, and factory handoff are related, but they are not interchangeable. A measurement report checks defined dimensions. CLO’s 3D view helps the team review silhouette and garment behavior under the selected simulation conditions. A physical sample remains useful when a decision depends on the sewn garment, material, movement, or construction. Factory approval also depends on the files and process your production partners require.

## What should stay consistent during a CLO grading trial?

A useful trial starts with the same approved inputs and acceptance criteria throughout. That way, the team can focus on the pattern and rules rather than sorting out differences in the source data.

1. **Choose one approved base pattern.** Use the pattern and base size approved for the style. Keep piece names, seam allowances, notches, internal lines, and construction details consistent.
2. **Freeze the size specification.** Use the same size labels and target finished-garment measurements. State whether each point measures a flat garment, a circumference, or another defined method, and record the unit.
3. **Write down the grade rules.** Document the increment at each relevant point, including rules that change between size intervals. Keep direction and sign clear.
4. **Align points of measure.** Give each point a shared name, location, and method. Keep body-measurement landmarks distinct from garment-measurement points.
5. **Set acceptance limits before grading.** Assign a target and tolerance to each point. Flag critical areas—such as closures, movement zones, or seams that join pattern pieces—for additional review.
6. **List the handoff outputs.** Specify the pattern files, size naming, measurement chart, production information, and review images the pattern room or factory actually needs.

Keep a trial record with these inputs and the resulting files. If a setup adjustment is needed, record it while keeping the approved pattern, target measurements, and acceptance limits unchanged. This makes the evaluation easier to repeat after a rule edit or at a later review.

CLO’s documented product information describes pattern and measurement capabilities. Use the trial to confirm how the current setup handles your own pattern, rules, measurements, and handoff needs. Product documentation and your measured results work together: one describes capabilities, and the other shows how the workflow performs on your style.

## How should the team check every graded size?

Check every required size against the same target chart and tolerance rules. Include the smallest and largest sizes as well as every intermediate size, then review how connected pieces and garment proportions develop across the range.

1. **Generate the specified sizes.** Apply the approved rules to the selected base pattern and confirm the generated labels and size count match the production brief.
2. **Compare every specified measurement.** Measure each defined point in each size. Record the target, observed value, difference, and pass status in one audit table.
3. **Review pattern relationships.** Check joined seam lengths, notches, dart or pleat placement, closure alignment, and curves where pieces meet. Look closely at the ends of the size range and scan intermediate sizes for abrupt changes.
4. **Resolve and retest exceptions.** Check whether a mismatch comes from a grade rule, point definition, pattern geometry, or source specification. Make the approved correction, regenerate the sizes, and repeat the affected checks.
5. **Use 3D review for visual questions.** Choose an avatar and relevant base layers, and use the best available fabric-property information. Review balance, ease, silhouette, and movement areas across representative sizes.
6. **Confirm physical and production approval where needed.** Use a sewn sample or the team’s established approval process for decisions that depend on actual material, construction, movement, or factory execution.

A useful audit table includes style and pattern version, size, measurement point, target, observed value, difference, tolerance, disposition, and reviewer. When a point fails, the record helps show whether the issue affects one size, a rule across several sizes, or the measurement definition.

For a simple illustration, if the approved target is 70.0 cm and the assumed tolerance is plus or minus 0.4 cm, results from 69.6 cm through 70.4 cm fall within that point’s assumed limit. Apply the team’s correction procedure to any result outside the approved range.

Keep dimensional and visual evidence separate. The measurement audit shows whether defined points meet their limits. A 3D review records how the garment looks and behaves under the selected avatar, fabric, and simulation conditions. For each image, note the avatar, base layers, fabric-property record, and garment size so the team can repeat the setup.

A complete review set can include graded pattern files, a point-of-measure chart, an exceptions log, selected 3D views, and physical fit notes where required. CLO brings pattern work and garment visualization together; keeping the supporting records distinct makes each approval decision clearer.

## How does CLO support a fashion grading workflow?

CLO is designed for fashion and apparel development, combining 2D pattern creation and editing with 3D garment simulation. This connection lets a team work on patterns, assess a garment visually, and return to the pattern as a style develops.

For a practical trial, use one approved base pattern and the team’s actual grade rules. Define the measurements needed for the specification, generate the required sizes, and compare each one with the target chart. Then review pattern relationships and use 3D views for additional visual context. This gives patternmakers and technical designers a shared view of measured results and the garment’s appearance.

CLO’s 3D review is valuable as part of a broader approval process, not as a substitute for every other check. Simulation can help a team assess fit and silhouette, while a measurement audit verifies defined dimensions and a physical sample can answer questions about real materials, movement, and construction.

## Frequently asked questions

Does a 3D fit review prove that every graded measurement is correct?

No. A 3D view supports visual review, while a measurement chart checks defined points against approved dimensions. Keep both results when the project needs both checks.

Which sizes should the team inspect during a grading trial?

Check every required size against the target chart. Give added visual attention to the smallest and largest sizes, and inspect intermediate sizes for abrupt changes in shape or proportion.

Can a standard body-size chart replace the finished-garment specification?

No. Body-measurement references describe the wearer; a finished-garment specification gives the pattern team its target garment measurements and acceptance limits. Keep the two clearly labeled.

What should the team keep from a grading trial?

Keep the source pattern, grade rules, size labels, measurement chart, exceptions log, review images, and any required physical fit notes together. These records make it easier to understand and repeat the checks.
